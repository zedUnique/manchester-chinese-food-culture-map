# Research-Led Regional Foodway Framework

## Status of the 13 Entries

The map does not claim that China has an official set of thirteen cuisines. Its thirteen markers are a curated analytical sample designed for this project's research question: how can Manchester Chinese restaurants help non-Chinese users understand the regional, historical and social diversity behind Chinese food?

The framework combines three ideas:

1. Chinese food culture has meaningful geographical differentiation.
2. The canonical Four or Eight Great Cuisines are influential, but they are not exhaustive.
3. Restaurant POIs can be used as cultural evidence, provided that the categories and data limitations are stated clearly.

The map markers are therefore cultural entry points, not formal administrative boundaries or a new official cuisine classification.

## Selection Criteria

An entry is retained when it meets at least one of the following criteria:

- it is a recognised regional cuisine or regional foodway in academic literature;
- it communicates a distinctive environment, migration history, ethnic or urban food culture to non-Chinese users;
- it has evidenced representation in Manchester restaurants;
- its absence from the recorded Manchester restaurant landscape is itself a useful and clearly labelled research gap.

Adjacent traditions are merged only where the literature supports continuity and the combined entry improves communication. Restaurant labels are kept as a separate evidence layer and may overlap because a single Manchester restaurant can serve food from several regions.

## The 13 Project Entries

| Project entry | Academic anchor | Project adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sichuan-Chongqing | Chuan | Zhu et al. place Sichuan and Chongqing within the same Chuan category. The project uses mala, hotpot and fermented seasonings to explain shared foodways without claiming that the two places are identical. |
| Shandong | Lu | Retained as a canonical northern cuisine and as an important influence on northern and historical court cooking. |
| Guangdong-Cantonese | Yue | Retained because Cantonese food is central to overseas Chinese restaurant history and Manchester Chinatown. |
| Hong Kong foodways | HK | Kept separate from Yue as an urban and migration-focused entry. Zhu et al. also analyse Hong Kong separately; the project focuses on cha chaan teng, roast-meat and diasporic food memory. |
| Jiangsu-Zhejiang | Su and Zhe | Merged for this interface because later regional-cuisine research notes substantial similarity between Jiangsu and Zhejiang. Huaiyang and Jiangnan remain explanatory cultural terms within the entry. |
| Anhui | Hui | Retained so that the framework does not omit one of the canonical Eight Great Cuisines. With no verified Manchester POI, it is shown as a cultural reference and explicit fieldwork gap. |
| Fujian | Min | Retained for mountain-coastal ecology, soup traditions and maritime migration, even though a verified Manchester POI is not yet recorded. |
| Hunan | Xiang | Retained to distinguish fresh and sour chilli traditions from Sichuan-Chongqing mala. |
| Beijing | Jing | Retained because city-level culinary identities can be culturally meaningful and because Beijing dishes are evidenced on Manchester menus. |
| Northeast | Dongbei | Retained for cold-climate stews, pickling, large shared portions and frontier influences that are not represented by the Eight Great Cuisines alone. |
| Northwest | Xibei | The literature groups Shaanxi, Gansu, Qinghai and Ningxia as Xibei. The current Manchester evidence is Shaanxi-led, so the interface states this focus instead of generalising it to the whole Northwest. |
| Xinjiang foodways | Xinjiang / Muslim foodways | Retained for Uyghur and other Muslim food cultures, lamb, wheat foods, oasis agriculture and Silk Road exchange. The absence of a verified central-Manchester POI is shown as a research gap. |
| Yunnan-Guizhou | Yungui | Zhu et al. group Yunnan and Guizhou as Yungui. The current content is Yunnan-led, so Guizhou representation is explicitly marked for later research. |

## Academic Basis

- Chen Chuankang (1994) treats Chinese diet and food culture as geographical phenomena with regional differentiation: [The Culture of Chinese Diet: Regional Differentiation and Developing Trends](https://www.geog.com.cn/CN/10.11821/xb199403004).
- Zhu et al. (2013) analyse approximately twenty regional cuisines and show that geographical proximity is strongly related to cuisine similarity. Their dataset includes Chuan, Lu, Yue, Su, Zhe, Min, Xiang, Jing, Dongbei, Hong Kong, Xibei, Xinjiang and Yungui: [Geography and Similarity of Regional Cuisines in China](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0079161).
- Zhang and Ma (2020) explain regional cuisine through geography, environment, economy, culture, ethnicity, religion, ingredients and cooking methods. They also note the close relationship between Jiangsu and Zhejiang food cultures: [Nutritional Characteristics and Health Effects of Regional Cuisines in China](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s42779-020-0045-z).
- King (2020) introduces Chinese culinary regionalism as a broader field of study, supporting the project's decision not to treat the canonical Four or Eight Great Cuisines as an exhaustive account of Chinese food culture: [Special Issue on Chinese Culinary Regionalism: Introduction](https://doi.org/10.1080/20549547.2020.1770490).
- Jiang et al. (2021) demonstrate how restaurant POI data can support food-culture regionalization, while their method also makes clear that the resulting structure depends on the selected dataset and categories: [Using Restaurant POI Data to Explore Regional Structure of Food Culture Based on Cuisine Preference](https://www.mdpi.com/2220-9964/10/1/38).

Guo Cheng's dissertation is also used as a local methodological warning: cultural POI categories may operate at different scales and should be normalised before they are presented as one filter system. This project responds by separating the map framework from Manchester restaurant evidence.

## Dissertation-Ready Rationale

This project does not reproduce the Eight Great Cuisines as a closed taxonomy, nor does it propose an official thirteen-cuisine system. Instead, it uses thirteen research-led regional foodway entries as an interpretive interface. Every canonical Eight Great Cuisine tradition remains visible, although Jiangsu and Zhejiang are combined as a clearly labelled Su-Zhe entry. The framework then adds Beijing, Northeast, Hong Kong, Northwest, Xinjiang and Yunnan-Guizhou because they communicate urban, ethnic, environmental and migratory dimensions that an Eight Cuisines interface would omit. The entries are adapted primarily from the broader regional categories analysed by Zhu et al. (2013), then narrowed or merged according to documented culinary continuity, Manchester restaurant evidence and the needs of non-Chinese users. Restaurant labels form a separate, overlapping evidence layer and are assigned only through menus, restaurant self-description, public records or fieldwork. The resulting framework is therefore a transparent cultural-mapping method rather than a claim that China has one fixed or universally accepted set of thirteen cuisines.

## Limitations

- The thirteen entries are a selected interface, not a complete account of Chinese food culture.
- Current Manchester representation is uneven and reflects the available restaurant evidence.
- Northwest and Yunnan-Guizhou currently use Shaanxi-led and Yunnan-led content respectively.
- Restaurant menus can be multi-regional, adapted to local markets and subject to change.
- The framework should be revisited after fieldwork, interviews and final restaurant verification.
